The first A/B test I ran at OmniRetail had a 94% confidence interval after two weeks. We shipped the variant. Three months later we couldn't find any evidence the change had moved the metric we were optimising for.

That experience started a longer investigation into why experimentation programs produce so many local maxima — and so few real insights.

## The confirmation bias problem

Most tests are designed to confirm something the team already believes. A redesigned CTA button. A reordered form. A different headline on a landing page. These are fine things to test, but they're usually chosen because the team *expects* them to win — and an experiment designed to confirm a belief is statistically likely to do so, especially when you stop it as soon as you hit significance.

The more useful framing is adversarial: what would falsify our belief about this user behaviour? A test designed to disprove a hypothesis generates more signal than one designed to confirm it.
